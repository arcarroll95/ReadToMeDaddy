**Interventional psychiatry is best understood as two tracks.** The **urgency track** is for illness that is dangerous now: psychotic depression, catatonia, life-threatening refusal of food or fluids, and severe suicidality. Here ECT, or sometimes ketamine, is chosen first because the treatment needs to be fast and reliable. The **resistance track** is for depression that has failed about two adequate medication trials. Here TMS, esketamine or ketamine, and pharmacologic augmentation compete, and ECT stays available..[1][2][3]

What each intervention has proven:

- **ECT** remains the most reliably effective acute treatment, especially for inpatients, older adults, and psychotic depression. The two head-to-head trials against ketamine only partly agree: in inpatients ECT gave higher remission than ketamine (63% vs 46%), while in nonpsychotic, mostly outpatient TRD ketamine was noninferior.[4][3][5]
- **Esketamine** is the only FDA-approved glutamatergic treatment. Its indications are TRD, alone or with an oral antidepressant, and depressive symptoms in MDD with acute suicidal ideation. Its label states that it has **not** been shown to prevent suicide.[6][7]
- **IV racemic ketamine** is legal off-label practice, not an approved psychiatric treatment.[8]
- **TMS** is FDA-cleared. Theta-burst protocols took sessions from 37.5 minutes to about 3 minutes with equal outcomes.[9][10]
- **Psilocybin** has positive phase 2 trials alongside one 2026 phase 2b trial that missed its primary endpoint. It remains **investigational, federally Schedule I, and available only through research**.[11][12][13]

**1. The clinical problem: when interventions enter**

**Defining treatment resistance.** No single definition is universally accepted:

- **Common research and regulatory definition:** failure of at least two antidepressants at adequate dose for at least 4–6 weeks each. "Failure" usually means less than 25–50% improvement.[1]
- **Delphi consensus for trials:** at least two failures with less than 25% improvement. Partial response (25% to under 50%) is a separate category, "partially responsive depression," which can be diagnosed after a single trial.[2]
- **How variable the field is:** Of 155 published definitions, only about half required two failures. Many "TRD" trials enrolled patients after just one failed treatment, so trial populations are not interchangeable.[2]
- **Guideline language differs:** NICE dropped "treatment resistance" as pejorative in favor of stepped care, and the APA guideline speaks of partial responders and non-responders.[14]
- **Eligibility for interventions depends on the definition.** Esketamine trials required 25% or less improvement on two or more antidepressants, documented by questionnaire and records. They excluded psychosis, bipolar disorder, recent moderate-to-severe substance use, and recent suicidality.[15]
- **Bipolar depression has its own definition.** The ISBD task force (2025) defines it as an inadequate response (less than 50% reduction) to two approved bipolar-depression treatments, such as quetiapine, lurasidone, olanzapine/fluoxetine, cariprazine, or lumateperone, each given at adequate dose and duration.[16]

**Rule out pseudo-resistance first.** Before labeling depression "resistant," check for:

- Nonadherence.
- Misdiagnosis, especially unrecognized bipolar disorder.
- Comorbid anxiety, substance use, or personality disorder.
- Inadequate dose or duration.[1]

After one or two failures, standard options are raising the dose, switching, combining antidepressants, or augmenting with an atypical antipsychotic, lithium, or a stimulant-type agent.[1]
**2. Ketamine and esketamine**

**Mechanism: what is established and what is inferred.** Ketamine is a noncompetitive NMDA receptor antagonist. Esketamine, the S-enantiomer, binds NMDA receptors with 3–4 times the affinity of arketamine, the R-enantiomer.[8]
